On March 10, 2026, I took part in the **Vibe Coding Hackathon**, organized by Howest. It was a full day in which teams had to go from a vague idea to a working application. The fact that the event sold out immediately showed how much interest there was.

Unlike the previous Tech&Meet evenings, this was not a passive listening session, but a hands-on competition. The central concept was **vibe coding**: building software by guiding AI models such as Claude, GPT-4o, or Gemini through natural language instead of writing every line of code yourself. The event slogan summed it up well: you provide the vision, the AI does the heavy lifting.

The target audience was unusually broad: both IT students and business students were explicitly addressed. For IT students, the role shifts from bricklayer to architect: you focus on logic, security, and integrations. For business students, vibe coding opens a door that was often closed before: with tools like **Lovable**, you can build your own interfaces and flows without a technical background. That part was honestly one of the coolest ideas of the day, because you can go from idea to something real much faster.

Teams could choose from three tracks: a B2B automation solution (The Paperwork Killer), a local consumer app (The Hyper-Local Hero), or an interactive data dashboard (The Data Storyteller). The rule was clear: no PowerPoints, only a working demo by the end of the day. That pressure made it exciting and realistic.

What I learned goes beyond technical skills. Vibe coding forces you to think clearly about what you actually want to build: vague instructions produce vague results. It was an exercise in problem analysis, communication, and iterative thinking, all competencies that are sometimes underemphasized in education compared with pure coding knowledge.

At the same time, the rise of vibe coding also raises questions that the day itself did not fully answer: what about code quality, maintainability, and the security of AI-generated code? As an IT student, I think that critical layer is what separates us from someone who just works with prompts. It would be great if the program leaned into that more, for example by combining vibe coding with lessons on code review and technical debt.

Would I attend again? Absolutely. It was an energetic, inspiring day that broadened my view of software development. I would also strongly recommend it to fellow students, both from IT and business: that mix of profiles made the collaboration and conversations interesting and educational.
